## The headline

**Lowering the dock price makes purses WORSE, not better.** Your instinct was that cheaper crates
would leave captains with less spare change — the opposite happens: a cheaper crate means every
purchase eats less of a captain's purse, so more coin sits there unspent. The share of games where
somebody ends up holding over 10 coins jumps from about half (52%) today to about six in seven (85%)
under your proposed 1–3 dock range.

**Raising the battle cost to 4 barely moves purses, and prices a few more captains out of ever
affording a fight they wanted.** It does cut how often ships fight (down about a third), but the
coin it saves doesn't show up in anyone's purse, because a battle's prize was never coins — it's a
crate. Today, about 1 captain in 50 never once affords a fight or a crate they had reason to want,
across a whole voyage; at battle cost 4, that rises to about 1 in 19.

**Neither of your two levers, alone or together, gets purses under control.** The dock-price lever
moves the number in the wrong direction; the battle-cost lever barely moves it at all. Read on for
the numbers themselves and what they leave open.

## What the numbers do not tell you

- **This is bot play, not your table.** All four strategies are the shipping AI captains
  (`pirate`, `trader`, `balanced`, `rusher`), not a human weighing a purchase the way you or a
  friend would. A human captain who *chooses* to hoard coins would move these numbers in ways no
  bot here will.
- **"Worst purse anyone held" is a peak, not a typical outcome** — it is the single richest moment
  across 300 games × 4 seats × every turn of the voyage. The median (the "typical purse" column) is
  the more honest read of an ordinary captain's ordinary turn.
- **Neither candidate was tuned — only the two values you named were tried.** There may be a setting
  between today's and your candidates, or a different number entirely (`passCoin`, `dockHeads`,
  `dockTails`), that does what you actually want. This table answers "do your two ideas work,"
  not "what would."
- **The proxy for "felt able to act" is a judgement call, not a measurement of feeling.** It only
  catches two of the game's several actions (attacking, buying a needed crate) — not trading,
  fishing (v2 rule 3 already removed that), or simply sailing somewhere. A captain who never wanted
  to fight or buy in a given voyage reads as "never boxed out" by this proxy, correctly, but that
  captain also never got a chance to be blocked.

## His target metric, in his words

*"On a captain's own turn, was there something in reach worth doing (an adjacent rival worth
robbing, or a dock selling a crate their recipe still needs) that they could not afford? Tallied per
captain per whole voyage — did that captain have a reason to act and never once, the entire game,
could afford it."* **A captain who meets that description is "locked out."** Two readings of "worth
robbing," because they can disagree:

- **any** — the rival is holding *any* crate at all (the reading the first matrix used, called
  "boxed out" there).
- **need** — the rival is holding a crate *this captain's own recipe still needs* (the stricter,
  more honest reading of "worth" — robbing someone with nothing useful to your bakery isn't really a
  reason, even though the engine legally allows it).

Reported per row: **share of GAMES with at least one locked-out captain**, **mean locked-out
captains per game**, and the **distribution** (how many games had 0, 1, 2, 3, or all 4 captains shut
out) — not the per-player-game rate the first matrix reported, because a designer reading "12% of
player-games" cannot tell whether that's one bad game in eight or one bad captain scattered thinly
across all of them. The per-game share answers that directly.

## What each one does, in plain English, and the recommendation

**Lever A — halve the battle cost (`powder` 2 → 1). Recommended.** Cuts the locked-out rate
**roughly in half** on both readings (8.0%→4.0%, 12.0%→7.7%) and leaves the purse-ceiling problem
essentially untouched (52.3%→54.1% — a rounding-sized move, not a regression). It also makes fights
MORE common (3.56→4.18 per game) without dragging voyages out (14.93→14.85 days). **This is the
best-performing lever in the whole matrix against your own target metric, and it does the least
damage to anything else.** This is the one to build, pending your sign-off.

**His requested change — the dock flip, 2/5 → 1/3. Recommend against, alone.** It is the single
best fix for the purse-ceiling problem (52.3%→15.3%) but it makes the affordability problem you
actually described nearly TWICE as common (8.0%→14.3%, 12.0%→23.0%) — the opposite of what "run the
numbers" was asked to check. If the purse ceiling still needs fixing after Lever A ships, a **smaller**
dock-payout nudge (not fully halved) measured on top of Lever A is the more promising combination —
not measured in this pass, flagged here as the next question.

**Lever C — double the starting purse (`startCoins` 3 → 6). Recommend against, as the primary
fix.** Helps the locked-out rate about as much as Lever A (8.0%→5.3%, 12.0%→7.7%) but makes the
purse-ceiling problem meaningfully WORSE (52.3%→68.9%) — the same trade-off the first matrix found
with cheaper crates: more money up front means more of it sits unspent by the end. Could work as a
small supporting nudge (+1, not double) if Lever A alone doesn't fully close the gap — not measured
here.

**Lever B — halve the dry-shelf floor price (`blackMarket` 10 → 5). Recommend against — it doesn't
do what it looks like it should.** Counter-intuitively, the locked-out rate got slightly WORSE
(8.0%→9.3%, 12.0%→15.3%), not better. Lowering a price should only ever make a purchase MORE
affordable at that one moment — so this is a genuine, measured surprise, not a hunch. **The likely
mechanism, not fully traced down in this pass:** `blackMarket` isn't only a price a captain pays —
it's also read by the bots' OWN buying logic (`blackMarketPick`/`doDock`'s barter-vs-coins choice),
so changing it reshapes how bots play the *entire* voyage, not just the price of one dry-shelf
purchase. Flagging the mechanism as unconfirmed rather than asserting a story for it (CLAUDE.md
rule 6) — worth a dedicated look if this lever is ever reconsidered, but it is not the fix to build
today.

# 2026-08-21, later the same day — the second correction: it's a BAND, not a yes/no

**Wyatt, re-reading the section above:** *"i want players to be unable to afford a desirable action
AT LEAST ONCE but NOT MORE THAN 3 TIMES per game: a balanced economy gives them just enough money at
just the right time, most of the time -- so they value money, but don't squander it. my 1/3 dock
change seems to do this but i can't tell because your metrics are not what i intended."*

**The "locked-out" metric above (first correction, same day) only answered yes/no** — did a captain
get shut out for the ENTIRE voyage, never once affording a reason to act. That cannot distinguish a
captain priced out ONE time (probably healthy — money mattered, once) from a captain priced out NINE
times (money is a wall, not a texture). This section reports the metric he actually wants: **N**, a
count, not a boolean, and a **band**, not a threshold.

## The band metric, in his words

For each captain, in each game: **N = the number of that captain's own turns, across the whole
voyage, where a desirable action was in reach — his own definition, "adjacent rival with a crate, or
a dock selling a recipe-needed crate" — and they could not afford it.** A turn where both an attack
and a dock purchase were in reach and both unaffordable counts as **one** moment, not two — N counts
turns with a miss in them, not the number of separate things a captain wanted that turn.

- **Balanced = 1 ≤ N ≤ 3.** THE headline number, every row below: what share of captains land here.
- **N = 0 — money never bit.** Not necessarily bad, but not the thing he asked for either: this
  captain never once felt the pinch.
- **N ≥ 4 — money is stifling.** The economy is punishing this captain repeatedly, not teaching them
  once.

Both attack readings from the first correction are kept ("any crate nearby" / "a crate this
captain's own recipe needs") — "any" matches his restated definition verbatim and is the headline;
"need" is reported alongside, same reasoning as before.
