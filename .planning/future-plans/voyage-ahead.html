The voyage ahead

Twenty questions about where Pastry Pirates sails after launch: money, accounts, ways to play, merch and the real-world board. Each one is built from what comparable games actually do.

Answered 14 September. Your digest is just below, and your full words are saved in the repo. Follow-ups go one at a time in chat and you answer out loud. If you'd rather dictate a batch, start each answer with its number. Every "others" note links to the page it came from; anything that couldn't be verified was left out.

Already settled, so not asked again

  • It's "a living game I grow for years" (your ruling, 30 Aug).
  • Logins and paid digital merch come within a month of launch, so the questions ask what they're for, not whether.

  1. Stable on Cloudflare, playable by anyone, strong code base. “We’re getting close to that now.”
  2. Launched, with hundreds of daily players.
  3. Thousands of daily players, and some donations.

You asked for my thoughts: where bought ends and earned begins

Proposed rule: money buys what you wear; voyages earn what you’ve proven. When someone sees another captain’s ship and wonders “did they pay for that?”, the kind of thing it is should answer the question.

Buyable, with money or dubloons

  • Ship paint, sails you draw on, flags
  • Flippinator coin designs
  • Bakery decor and ovens
  • Pet outfits (your idea: earn the pet, buy its clothes)
  • Music packs
  • Expansion seas, shared with your crew

Earned only, never for sale

  • Recipes: yours once you bake one to a win
  • Trophies and titles
  • Pets, each tied to a feat, e.g. the candycrab for winning through a storm
  • Leaderboard rank and Daily Voyage streaks
  • The cookie flag, the one clout you can pay for, because it says “I backed the maker”, not “I’m good”

Three risks to design around. First: if dubloons can be both bought and earned, they must never buy anything in the earned-only column, or clout becomes buyable. Second: a captain could pile up dubloons sailing solo against bots all day, so earnings may need a daily cap. Third: paid mystery boxes are the legal hot spot (checked below). The simplest safe version: money buys named items at a clear price, never dubloons, and dubloons are only ever earned. That also closes the first risk.

Mystery boxes, checked at the source

  • Apple and Google Play both require showing the odds of every item before purchase. Apple 3.1.1 Google Play
  • Belgium (2018) found paid loot boxes to be illegal gambling, including boxes bought with in-game coins that were bought with money. Gaming Commission The Netherlands’ top court overturned its own regulator’s penalty against EA in 2022. Bird & Bird
  • UK (2022): not gambling law “at this time”, but children shouldn’t be able to buy them without a parent turning it on. GOV.UK
  • US (Jan 2025): the maker of Genshin Impact paid $20M. It may not sell loot boxes to under-16s without a parent’s consent, and must show odds and what its coins cost in real money. FTC
  • So: the thrill you like is safe as a mystery box you earn by playing. Selling them for money waits until a lawyer has looked, and depends on your answer about kids.

Your architecture question, in plain English

  • Each world is a folder. Pasta Pirates lives in its own folder holding its islands, ingredients, recipes, captains, music, art and words. The game reads whichever folder is chosen and never mentions a world by name. Today’s pastry sea becomes the first folder. The term is “data-driven content packs”.
  • Why sessions stop colliding: a new world is new files in a new folder, and nobody edits a shared list. The art review and narration pass happen inside that folder.
  • Your ledger is the right idea. The standard term is “entitlements and inventory”: one record per captain of what they own, have earned, and hold. The game asks one question, e.g. “does anyone in this crew own Sushi Pirates?”, and your crew-sharing rule is just that question.
  • The catch: anyone can edit what their own browser says. So a captain must never be able to write their own trophies or purchases; a trusted server checks the receipt and writes the ledger. That needs accounts first.
  • Native apps keep one set of game files. A wrapper puts the game inside an app, with Capacitor for iPhone and Android and Tauri or Electron for Steam, and solo against bots works offline. Capacitor Apple lets a web purchase unlock on iPhone, as long as the iPhone app also sells that item. Apple 3.1.3(b) Whether Steam allows the same is not verified.
  • Order, smallest first: move today’s sea into a world folder with no visible change → add a tiny second test world without touching shared files → a free cosmetic slot (flip coins) → accounts and the real ledger → earned trophies → the store → the native apps. Paid mystery boxes and buyable dubloons are parked.

A cute pastry game can look like a kids' game to a regulator. US children's privacy law covers games aimed at under-13s, and counts a screen name as personal information. UK law requires the most private settings by default for children.

Families with young kids, adults, classrooms? And chat: none, preset pirate phrases, emoji, or typed?

What others do
  • Among Us: under-13s need a parent's permission, and guests only get preset "Quick Chat". Innersloth

  • US rule in plain terms. FTC UK ICO
Your answerToo sophisticated for under-13s, and an art redesign is coming. Chat only among people who know each other; strangers get the board plus something like a high-five. No moderation.
