# v2 playtest — Wyatt, 2026-08-04

**STATUS: both findings fixed in the build, and the rules and PRD are in sync. See the bottom.**

First real play of the `v2/` build. Two findings, both structural, both confirmed in code.

---

## 1. The bots spam you with trade offers, and it feels like advertising

> *"When the bots try to trade every single round, the game completely stalls out and feels super
> boring because it just feels like spam. The bots ask to trade continuously. They have no memory of
> the fact that you didn't trade with them before."*

**Three causes, all in the v2 build, all mine:**

1. **The outcry is unconditional.** `engine.js` runs `yield* this.outcry(p)` at the top of every
   single turn for every captain. There is no gate at all — no cost, no cooldown, no "is this worth
   doing".
2. **The bot never reads its own refusal memory.** `engine.js` maintains `p.refused`, but
   `strategy.js` only ever consults it to decide whether to *attack* someone. The `offer` resolver
   ignores it completely, so a bot re-asks the same captain for the same crate every round forever.
3. **The memory is incomplete anyway.** A refusal is only recorded when *nobody* answered. Decline
   an offer while another bot answers it and nothing is recorded — so even the memory that exists
   misses most refusals.

And a fourth thing that makes it read specifically as *advertising* rather than negotiation: the bot
picks what to ask for by `crateValue()` alone, which returns the same answer turn after turn. So it
is not just asking too often — **it is asking for the same thing, in the same words, forever.**

### Wyatt's proposed fix: take trade out of the turn loop and make it an Act

> *"I'm wondering if an easier solution here is just to take trade out of the turn loop entirely and
> make it part of one of the actions that you have to expend, so that when you are crossing the seas,
> you have to use that dead time to trade."*

**I think this is right, and it fixes something else at the same time.** The measured action mix had
**40–57% of turns as passes** — a captain sails and does nothing, because docking needs a berth,
fighting needs an adjacency and the cast is once a round. Trade-as-an-action is exactly the thing to
put in that hole. Crossing the sea stops being dead time and becomes when you do business.

It also kills the spam *structurally* rather than by tuning: a bot that must give up a dock, a fight
or the cast to make an offer will almost never spend it frivolously. That is a much better guarantee
than a cooldown, because it needs no bot discipline to hold.

**Four consequences to design for, not reasons against:**

- **Every economy number in the ruleset was measured at ~35 trades a game.** As an action it will be
  a small fraction of that. Coins, crate prices and the treasure value all need re-measuring — the
  whole balance rests on trade volume.
- **Battles get rarer still.** They already sit at 1.6 a game, and they are licensed by *refusals*.
  Fewer offers means fewer refusals means fewer fights. It may go to near zero. The threat premium
  that governs this is in `BOT-STRATEGY.md` §4 and would need to go up.
- **Off-turn engagement mostly survives**, which is the good news. *Answering* an offer still happens
  on somebody else's turn, so the interesting half stays where it was.
- **Three things now compete for one slot** — dock, cast, trade. That is a healthier decision than
  the current "dock or pass", but it means the cast will be called less often too.

### The bot rule that is missing, and why it is a feature

Wyatt's own diagnosis is the right bot rule:

> *"They have to be able to infer that if you didn't trade with them before, then it's likely because
> you need the ingredient yourself."*

That is a real Bayesian read and it is cheap: keep a per-(captain, ingredient) refusal memory, and
treat a refusal as **evidence about their secret recipe**, not just as a cooldown. It should do two
things — stop asking, and raise the estimate that they need it.

**And that makes refusing a trade leak information**, which the game's hidden-recipe layer wants
anyway. A captain who won't sell cocoa has told the table something. That is not a patch on a bug;
it is the negotiation layer the game is supposed to have.

## 2. Most of what the engine does is invisible

> *"Many of the events in the game are not narrated, so the player can't see what the engine is
> doing… if we can't see that it is now someone else's turn and that they're deciding to do this,
> then we have no ability to really understand what's happening in the game. Zero."*

Confirmed exactly. The engine emits 21 event types; `describe()` in `v2/ui.js` narrates 18 and
returns `null` for three:

```
turn        ← never narrated. You cannot see whose turn it is.
setup       ← never narrated. Who drew which boat power is invisible.
gameover    ← never narrated.
```

**`turn` is the fatal one.** Every other line in the log is an action with no owner announced before
it, so the whole game reads as an undifferentiated stream. The v1 game had "X takes the wheel…" and
I dropped it without replacing it.

There is a second half to this that the event list does not show: the driver in `v2/main.js` only
re-renders the board for bot steps of kind `act`, `cast` and `commit`. A bot's **sail** and its
**offer** produce no redraw at the moment they happen, so even the narrated events can arrive
detached from the board state they describe.

**This is not a copy problem, it is the narration tier work from the PRD (§6) never having been
done.** The build ships a single flat tier — one line per event, same weight for everything — which
is exactly what that section says not to do. A turn header is a **Beat**; a sail is a **Ticker**.
Without the tiers there is no way to make "it is now Gingersnap's turn" louder than "Gingersnap
holds course", so everything reads at once and therefore nothing reads at all.

---

## What this says about the build overall

Both findings are in the layer I built fastest and tested least. Neither is in the engine or the
ruleset — the rules themselves were never really exercised, because the presentation made it
impossible to see them working. **A future session should not read "the self-test plays 200/200
games" as evidence that the game is playable.** It is evidence that the rules resolve.


---

## Fixed, 2026-08-04

**Trade is now an Act**, not a free phase. Ruleset §3 and the PRD are updated to match.

**The bots remember.** A refusal is recorded per (captain, ingredient) and read in three places: the
bot will not re-ask someone who turned it down for that crate, it raises its estimate of what they
will charge, and it treats the refusal as evidence they need it themselves.

**`v2/events.js` is the single source of truth.** 24 event types, each declared once with its
fields, tier and narration line. `emit()` throws on an unregistered type; the self-test narrates
every event of every game and fails if any comes back empty. The Trawler's survival and the
Gambler's extra rung now carry their *reason*, so they read as what they are instead of as a number.

Measured after the change, 150 games:

```
                     before        after
trades per game        ~35          17.2
turns doing nothing   40-57%       ~20%     (trade fills the transit turn)
battles per game       1.6          0.81
every emitted type narrates          yes — gated in the self-test
```

**Two things got worse and are not fixed:**

- **Battles fell to 0.81 a game.** Fewer offers means fewer public refusals means fewer licences to
  fight. A second licence was added — *desperation*, fighting for a crate that is off the board —
  which recovered it from 0.32, but it is still thin for a pirate game.
- **The seat spread widened to ~16 points** (16.0 / 24.7 / 27.3 / 32.0), favouring later seats. It
  was ~9 before. Unexplained, and it should be chased before anyone calls this balanced.

## Battles retuned, 2026-08-04

> *"This is a pirate game; battles should not be a last resort, they should be a viable strategy,
> period."* — Wyatt

**The cause was ordering, not caution.** Trade was evaluated first in the `act` resolver and returned
immediately, so a fight was only ever reached when no deal existed. Instrumented over 9,476 turns:

```
a laden rival was orthogonally adjacent:   46.5% of turns
...and the bot chose to fight:              1.4% of turns
```

The opportunity was there on nearly half of all turns and the bot took it on one in seventy.

**Fixed by weighing the two against each other** rather than ordering them: value the prize, size the
stake to beat what they will likely show, estimate the win chance from the coins and the wind, and
take whichever of fight-or-deal is worth more.

```
                   before      after
battles per game     0.81       7.33     (shipped v1 is 6.0)
trades per game      17.2       17.7
rounds per game      14.5       17.9
```

### ⚠️ And it broke turn-order balance badly

```
wins by seat:   12.0%   27.3%   20.0%   38.7%      ← a 26.7-point spread
```

Going last is now hugely better, and this is the *third* time the seat spread has moved with a
combat change. The likely mechanism is real rather than a bug: **when fighting is viable, acting
last is worth a great deal** — you see every position before choosing, and the first captain is
exposed to three attackers before acting again.

That is a genuine design consequence of making battle viable, and it is unresolved. It may need the
reverse-order power draft that was set aside earlier, or a rule that protects a captain who has just
been attacked. **Nobody should call this balanced.**

## The principle behind all of it, 2026-08-04

> *"Don't give bots gates to follow, give them logic-based rules that a human player would also be
> using."* — Wyatt

Every problem in this document is one gate:

- the offer at the top of every turn with no cost — a gate on *when*, not a judgement of *whether*
- attack only after a public refusal — a gate that made a 46.5% opportunity into a 1.4% action
- trade checked before battle and returning immediately — a gate hidden in line ordering

**Gates cannot be seen failing.** They emit no error and no strange number; the action simply never
happens and the game looks like it was designed that way. All three needed a human to play the build
before anyone noticed.

The `act` resolver is now a valuation — price every legal action in coins, take the maximum — and
the gates are gone from `buy`, `offer`, `cast` and `battle` with it. Written up as
`BOT-STRATEGY.md` §0, which now governs the rest of that document.

```
                  gates      valuation
battles per game   0.81        6.75
trades per game    17.2        22.0
casts per game      3.1         6.3
rounds per game    14.5        18.2
```

Every action type is now in genuine use. **The seat spread did not move** (12.7 / 22.0 / 26.0 /
37.3), which supports the reading that it is a consequence of combat being viable rather than an
artefact of how the bots choose.

## The dock treasure flip, 2026-08-04

> *"Already found a bug: when you dock, you're supposed to flip a coin to find treasure, before you
> buy. That doesn't happen."* — Wyatt

**The flip was happening. Nothing about it was.** `resolveAct` flipped, credited the coins and
emitted `treasure` before yielding the buy — the rules were right and had always been right. What
the player got was a `Buy Cocoa for 4🌕?` prompt, and one 260ms line in a side panel he was not
looking at. So the correct answer to "does the flip happen" is: **not in any sense that matters.**

That is the second time on this build that a thing the engine does perfectly has counted as not
happening, which is the same lesson as the narration finding above and worth stating as a rule:
**a rule that resolves invisibly has not been implemented.**

**Fixed as a beat, not a line.** Docking now yields a `flip` request before the coin is turned:
a socket and a spinning coin in the action panel, tapped by the captain, held on the face for a
second, and only then does the buy prompt exist. The engine still calls `this.flip()` itself, before
the yield, so the order of RNG calls never depends on who is resolving — the outcome rides along in
the request purely so the reveal can be shown rather than announced. The response is discarded; it
is a beat, not a decision. Bots resolve it instantly and it narrates for them as it always did.

**Two more things fell out of chasing it:**

- **Every purchase was narrated as unwanted.** `buy` declares a `needed` field, the engine emitted
  the event without it, and `emit()` fills a declared-but-absent field with `null` — so the line
  read *"…and doesn't need it…"* on every crate anyone ever bought, including crates they needed
  most. The registry's null-filling is right; not passing the field was mine. Note the shape of it:
  the registry made the event *narratable*, it cannot make it *true*.
- **The artifact build was three hand-run steps and one of them was judgement.** It is
  `scripts/v2/assemble.py` now, which fails loudly if the flip art is not inlined, if the setup
  buttons are not wired, or if the event registry is missing — the last being how the page shipped
  once without it.

**Left alone deliberately:** you still cannot dock at an island whose crates are gone, so a stripped
island offers no treasure flip either. Changing that hands every captain a free +2 EV anywhere on the
map and would need re-measuring. It is a rules question for Wyatt, not a bug.

## The trade market, 2026-08-04 (second play)

Three findings. The middle one is worse than reported.

### 1. You could only ever bid yourself DOWN

> *"The options for when someone offers you a deal are to undercut their own offer TO YOU, which
> makes no sense. Undercutting only makes sense if you're trying to steal a deal from someone
> else — you should be able to propose that they spend more, not less."* — Wyatt

Biscotti offers 5🌕 for your milk and the buttons read *I'll take 1 / 2 / 3 / 4*. The ladder was
`[1,2,3,4,6,8].filter(n => n <= offer.giveCoins)` — the caller's opening number was a hard ceiling on
what anyone could ever get. **And it bound the bots too:** `strategy.js` refused outright whenever
its reservation exceeded the offer, rather than saying what it would take. A whole side of the
negotiation did not exist for anybody.

**Now the call is an opening bid.** An answer may come in above it, up to what is actually in the
caller's purse — which is public, so the ladder stops exactly where their money does. Undercutting
survives, but **only when another captain also holds one**, with the helper text saying which case
you are in. Sole holder sees accept-or-hold-out; against a rival you get both directions.

The three cases, observed in the browser at 390px:

```
sole holder, caller has 5      Aye - 5           (no counters; they cannot pay more)
rival holds one, caller has 10 Aye - 9  Nay - 10  Undercut 8  Undercut 7
sole holder, caller has 19     Aye - 5  Nay - 6 / 7 / 9 / 13
```

**And a fourth thing fell out of it:** `case "pick"` minimised the ask unconditionally, so a captain
crying a **sale** handed their crate to whoever bid *least*. Selling now takes the highest bid.

### 2. The refusal memory was never written to. At all.

> *"All bots should see that I denied a deal and not offer the same terms as that again. Eg one
> offered 5 for my milk; I said no. Another bot then offered 5 for my milk."*

`p.refusedFor` was initialised in `engine.js` and read in `strategy.js` and **never written by
anything, anywhere.** So `wasRefused()` was always false and `believedToNeed()` was always false.
This document records it as fixed earlier the same day. It was not — it was wired at both ends and
connected in the middle to nothing, which is the failure mode a registry catches for events and
nothing was catching for state.

**It should never have been per-bot in the first place.** The outcry is public; the whole table
watched you refuse. It is `game.floors` now — a table-level ledger of the highest price each captain
has publicly turned down for each crate — plus `game.swapsRefused` for pairs, which carry no price.

**A refusal stands until somebody beats it.** The engine does not re-put a question the table
already has the answer to, which is a rule about the game rather than an instruction to the bots. And
because a floor raises what every captain believes you will charge, the next offer for that crate is
*necessarily higher* than the one refused — "don't offer the same terms again" arrived at by pricing
rather than by a rule forbidding it.

Measured over 200 games against a captain who refuses everything:

```
                                                    before   after
answer prompts they had to sit through               1238      844
  ...at or under a price they already refused         612        0
  ...re-proposing a swap pair already refused         798        0
answers pitched above the call (did not exist)          0       25
undercuts offered with no rival to undercut           all        0
sales that took the lowest bid                        all        0
```

The swap channel is the part worth remembering: fixing coin offers first made **swaps 798 of the
remaining 1131 prompts**, because a swap has no price and a price floor cannot bind it. Half a fix
looked like a whole one until the numbers were split by kind.

Full-game figures barely moved — 300 games: 21.1 trades, 6.77 battles, 18.0 rounds, 300/300 finish.
No-deals fell from 1.3 to 0.1 a game, which is the intended shape: bots that used to refuse now name
a price.

### 3. The log was below the fold

> *"Omg I am wrong — I didn't see the ui log at the bottom; the narrations are displaying correctly,
> I just didn't see them."*

Worth keeping precisely because nothing was broken. The narration worked; it was in the right-hand
column, which on a phone stacks below the vane and the captain cards, so a player looking at the
board and the buttons never saw it. **Invisible and absent are the same finding twice in two days.**

Moved directly under the board and cut to two lines, so it reads as commentary on what just moved
rather than a document to scroll.

### One more of the same shape, caught on the way past

`nodeal` declared a `refusers` field the engine never passed. `emit()` fills a declared-but-absent
field with `null`, `.length` threw on every one of them, and `narrate()` swallowed the error and
returned the literal string `[nodeal]` — which the self-test accepted, because it only asked whether
*something* came back. `narrate(e, ctx, strict)` now rethrows and the self-test passes strict.

**That is three events in two days that declared a field the engine never sent** (`buy.needed`,
`nodeal.refusers`, and `answer` before this change). The registry guarantees an event *can* be
narrated. It cannot guarantee the engine tells it the truth, and nothing yet checks that.

### Still open

- **4 offers in ~480** are called at a number the only holder has already refused, so nobody can
  answer and the caller wastes an action. Under 1%; not chased.
- **The seat spread did not move** — 14.0 / 23.7 / 24.3 / 36.7. Unchanged by any of this, which
  continues to point at combat rather than the market.

## Board, bubbles and the storm question, 2026-08-04 (third play)

### The islands were drawn from the wrong thing entirely

> *"Your islands look all wrong — the ingredients are not on top of them, the shapes are wrong, and
> it is not clear where one is supposed to sail."* — Wyatt

**One cause, three symptoms.** The generator builds islands out of **tetrominoes** and records
`shapeIdx`, `rot` and `flip` for each. `drawBoard()` took the art for `shapeIdx`, **ignored the
rotation and the flip**, and stretched it edge to edge across the island's bounding rectangle with
`preserveAspectRatio="none"`. So the green landmass on screen had almost nothing to do with the
squares you could actually sail to. The crates were then drawn on the *real* cells — which put them
beside the picture rather than on it — and the berth, which is a **water square next to the island**,
was a small brown jetty lost in open water.

**Cells are the truth, so cells are what gets painted now.** A sand ring (over-sized rects, which
overlap into a single outline) with the green land inside it, one crate icon per island square
greying out as the storehouse empties, and the berth drawn last as a gold-ringed square with the
stock count on it. **The shape on screen is the shape of the island by construction** and cannot
drift from it again — which the old approach could not promise, because two independent things had
to agree.

No new art, and the stretched shape PNGs are simply not used.

### The lines now speak from the ship

> *"As well as putting the narration lines below the board, can you write them in text boxes that are
> in the board, attached to the ship that they are coming from?"*

Every owned line appears as a bubble pinned to that captain's ship, in their colour; table-wide beats
— a round, a gale — take the middle of the board. The owner is **derived from the field names the
registry already uses** (`p`, then `a`, then `caller`) rather than declared per event, so a new event
gets a bubble without anyone remembering to ask for one.

The overlay is a sibling of the SVG and takes no pointer events, so it can never swallow a click on a
sail square.

**And it exposed an ordering bug.** `drive()` narrated *before* redrawing, which was invisible with a
log and obvious with bubbles: a ship spoke from where it used to be, and the vane read `now: —` while
the log announced the wind. Draw, then narrate, then ask.

### Docking is always allowed now

Gating the action on `tokens[berth] > 0` meant a captain blown onto a stripped island could not take
their turn there at all. It costs almost nothing to allow — the action is only worth the treasure
flip on a bare island, so any bot with a better option takes it: **0.2 bare-storehouse docks a game**
against 17.4 treasure flips.

Chasing it turned up a genuinely wrong valuation, unrelated to the rule: the bot priced docking at
zero whenever it could not afford the crate — **but the flip happens first, and heads pays 4.** A
captain three coins short is on even odds of affording it. That is the whole reason the treasure is
dug before the storehouse opens, and the bot did not know it.

### The storm is not a bug. The exemption is bigger than it looks.

> *"Maybe bug: I haven't seen a storm yet."*

Storms fire at the stated rate. Over 300 games:

```
games with a storm by round 5      188/300   (63%)
storms per game                    3.4
storms that moved NOBODY           14%
ships that actually moved          42%
```

**The rate is right; the exemption is what makes a gale feel rare.** A ship in a berth *or within one
square of Tortuga* rides it out — and those five home squares plus seven berths are where captains
spend most of their time. Add the log having been in the right-hand column, below the fold on a
phone, and a storm could fire three times without ever being seen.

Now it takes the middle of the board as a banner. **Not changed:** the sheltered-harbour rule itself.
It is a real design question — whether Tortuga's apron should be that safe — and it belongs to Wyatt,
not to a session fixing a visibility complaint.
